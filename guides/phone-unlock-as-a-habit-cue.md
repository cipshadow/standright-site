# Can I use unlocking my phone as a cue for a new habit?

{% include updated.html %}

Yes, if the habit stays small and private. You answer one check-in yourself, the answer stays on the phone, and the cue is something the phone already does. Unlocking is that cue. Attach one tap to it instead of adding a timer you have to notice.

The studies below counted unlocks. They did not count posture, and they did not test a habit app.

## How often the unlock already happens

Two Android studies measured this and did not land on the same number.

[Hintze, Scholz, Findling, Mayrhofer, and Muaaz (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1145/2638728.2641697) used Device Analyzer logs. They kept phones rather than tablets, required a lock screen, and kept devices with at least seven fully recorded days. That left 1,960 Android phones. Unlocked use on those phones averaged 25 times a day. The median was 20, so the middle phone in the set sat below the average, which a minority of heavy users pull up.

[Harbach, von Zezschwitz, Fichtner, De Luca, and Smith (2014)](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/soups2014/soups14-paper-harbach.pdf) logged unlocks on people's own Android phones. They recruited 57 people in Hannover and Munich and kept 52, mostly students, after dropping cases for a missed debrief, a changed clock, or a failed log. Each person contributed four weeks, of which the authors analyzed 27 complete days, averaging within the person first. The mean was 47.8 unlocks a day, the standard deviation 26.4, and the median 42.1. The paper says this does not generalize to any particular population. In that group, use rose through the morning and fell after 9 pm.

A log of 1,960 phones and a field study of 52 phones disagree with each other. Both still put unlocking at tens of times a day. Use the pair as a range, not as a fact about your own phone. Do not read either number as a slouch count.

## Why the cue does the work a timer does not

A timer helps only if you notice that it is time and then decide to act. That decision is the willpower part, and it has to be made again at every ring. Miss the ring, or see it and swipe it away, and there was no moment already underway for the habit to use.

An unlock is underway for some other reason. You were going to open the phone, and the check rides along. You made the decision once, when you turned the check on and chose the hours. [Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts, and Wardle (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) used situation cues rather than clock times for this reason: a situation can prompt the action, while a time has to be watched for. What that study measured, and what it does not claim about an app, is in [Build a posture habit in a few seconds a day](/guides/posture-habit/).

## Keep it to one tap

At a median of 20 unlocks a day, or of 42, the action has to be one tap. Opening an app, following a stretch, or stopping to make a plan is a second task on top of something you were already doing.

The tap is your report: upright or slouched. The phone is not deciding how you are sitting. A glow that marks an angle is marking the phone. Where you hold it is in [How to stop slouching at your phone](/guides/stop-slouching-at-your-phone/).

## Quiet hours

You do not need a question on every unlock. Harbach's group unlocked through the day and less so after 9 pm, and some unlocks are only a glance. Hintze separated those glances from unlocks: people often light the screen and never pass the lock.

Hours, a minimum gap, and a pause are how you keep the frequent cue and drop the rest. The same rate that makes unlocking useful will make a prompt tiresome if it cannot be quiet.
